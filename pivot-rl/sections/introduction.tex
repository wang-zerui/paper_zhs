\section{Introduction}

Post-training for long-horizon agentic capabilities---tool use, code editing, terminal interaction, web search---has a tension between efficiency and quality.
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is efficient but degrades out-of-domain (OOD) performance: even when training spans multiple agentic domains and hundreds of thousands of trajectories, SFT models show consistent OOD regression on general benchmarks~\citep{shenfeld2025rlsrazoronlinereinforcement, chen2025retainingdoingroleonpolicy, chu2025sftmemorizesrlgeneralizes}.
End-to-end reinforcement learning (E2E~RL) preserves OOD performance by staying on-policy, but requires full multi-turn rollouts with online environment interaction at every training step~\citep{shenfeld2025rlsrazoronlinereinforcement, chen2025retainingdoingroleonpolicy, chu2025sftmemorizesrlgeneralizes}.
We ask: \emph{is there a post-training paradigm that achieves the accuracy and OOD retention of E2E~RL without the full-trajectory rollout burden?}

Since agentic tasks produce environment feedback at each assistant turn, a natural first attempt is to perform on-policy RL at individual turn boundaries in existing expert trajectories, avoiding full-trajectory rollouts entirely.
However, this naive approach is ineffective.
As Figure~\ref{fig:rl_tau_reward_stddev} illustrates, the per-batch reward standard deviation under random turn sampling decays rapidly over training, indicating that most turns quickly cease to provide any gradient signal.
The downstream effect is correspondingly modest: on $\tau^2$-Bench, random turn-level RL reaches only 59.68 accuracy, barely above the 58.44 achieved by SFT, while selecting informative turns reaches 63.81.
This motivates a more selective approach: identifying the subset of turns that carry informative training signal---which we call \emph{pivots}.

PivotRL consumes existing expert trajectories---the same data used by SFT---and converts them into on-policy RL at the pivot level.
At each pivot, we sample completions from the current policy and score them by agreement with the expert continuation under domain-appropriate verifiers, so the reward captures whether an action is \emph{aligned} rather than whether it is \emph{identical}.
To identify informative pivots, we profile each turn under the initial policy and retain only those with mixed outcomes, prioritizing turns where the policy sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails.

We show that PivotRL corrects two limitations of SFT\@.
Rewriting the SFT gradient as a policy gradient reveals a token-level exact-match reward that ignores functionally equivalent actions, and an importance weight that induces distribution shift~\citep{wu2025generalization}.
PivotRL replaces the first with a turn-level functional-matching reward over a set of acceptable actions, and eliminates the second by sampling on-policy at each pivot.
We prove in Theorem~\ref{thm:kl-projection} that under this matching-style reward, the optimal policy is a KL projection of the reference onto an expert-matching constraint---it increases mass on acceptable actions while preserving the reference ranking elsewhere, explaining why PivotRL improves task accuracy without OOD degradation (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

We validate this analysis on Qwen3-30B-A3B across four agentic domains.
First, PivotRL achieves better in-domain accuracy and stronger OOD retention than SFT under the same data: in-domain gains average $+14.11$ versus $+9.94$ for SFT, while OOD change averages $+0.20$ versus $-9.48$ for SFT\@.
Specifically, $\tau^2$-Bench improves $44.35 \to 63.81$, SWE-Bench Verified $19.07 \to 32.67$, Terminal-Bench $5.42 \to 20.00$, and BrowseComp $2.50 \to 11.30$.
