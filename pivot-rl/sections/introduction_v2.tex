\section{Introduction\paragraph{Offline reward profiling.}
For each pivot candidate $(s,a^*) \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cand}}$, we sample
$M_{\mathrm{prof}}$ local rollouts under the frozen reference policy
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$:
\[
a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(M_{\mathrm{prof}})} \sim \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid s).
\]
Let $r^{(m)}(s)$ denote the reward assigned to the $m$-th rollout at state $s$
(e.g., the local verifier reward). We define the empirical mean reward and
empirical reward variance at $s$ as
\[
\hat{\mu}(s)
=
\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{prof}}}
\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\mathrm{prof}}} r^{(m)}(s),
\qquad
\hat{\sigma}^2(s)
=
\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{prof}}}
\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\mathrm{prof}}}
\bigl(r^{(m)}(s)-\hat{\mu}(s)\bigr)^2.
\]
Intuitively, $\hat{\mu}(s)$ is the reference-policy success rate at pivot state
$s$, and $\hat{\sigma}^2(s)$ measures whether rollouts at $s$ have mixed
outcomes.

We then define the filtered pivot sets as
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mixed}}
=
\left\{
(s,a^*) \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cand}}
:\;
\hat{\sigma}^2(s) > 0
\right\},
\]
and
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}
=
\left\{
(s,a^*) \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mixed}}
:\;
\hat{\mu}(s) < \lambda_{\mathrm{diff}}
\right\}.
\]}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have increasingly relied on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), which yields models strong on areas where the training data matches the distribution of the target area~\citep{chu2025sftmemorizesrlgeneralizes,wu2025generalization}. However, studies have shown clear weaknesses of SFT, of which includes catastrophic forgetting, where training on one distribution of data leads to the loss of capability in other distributions the model has been previously trained on \citep{shenfeld2025rlsrazoronlinereinforcement,chen2025retainingdoingroleonpolicy}. While a straightforward method of mitigating this effect is to train on a larger, broader set of SFT data \citep{luo2023empiricalCF,li2024revisitingCF,liu2025midtraining,lambert2025tulu3pushingfrontiers}, this approach contradicts learnings that the quality of SFT data is more important than quantity \citep{zhou2023limaalignment} and that SFT is more suitable as a jump start to the model \citep{chu2025sftmemorizesrlgeneralizes} rather than as a main driver.

Meanwhile, recent developments in Reinforcement Learning (RL) \citep{sutton1998reinforcement} have opened a new frontier for models in a variety of domains, including utilizing human feedback \citep{ouyang2022instructgpt} and verified rewards \citep{shao2024deepseekmath,Guo_2025} that scale with the experience gain of the model \citep{silver2025era}. With observations that RL does not suffer from the forgetting problem of SFT \citep{lu2025onpolicydistillation,shenfeld2025rlsrazoronlinereinforcement,chen2025retainingdoingroleonpolicy,chu2025sftmemorizesrlgeneralizes}, recent developments have increased the role of RL to be the driver of generalization and last-mile performance gain allowing its quick adoption as the final stage of training for many state-of-the-art models \citep{Guo_2025,qwen3technicalreport,kimiteam2025kimik2openagentic,5team2025glm45agenticreasoningcoding}.

In this paper, we step further from the observations about SFT and RL to question the utility of SFT in training models, especially in agentic capabilities. Many agentic tasks, such as conversational tool use, terminal use, software engineering, and search rely on heavy long horizon tool-calling. The common approach to training models for these capabilities has been SFT \citep{prabhakar2025apigenmtagenticpipelinemultiturn,qin2023toollLM,xu2023toolbench,liu2025toolacewinningpointsllm,pan2025trainingsoftwareengineeringagents,nakano2021webgpt}, empowered by long-horizon benchmarks spanning conversational tool use, terminal interaction, and web browsing/search \citep{yao2024taubench,barres2025tau2bench,tbench_2025,wei2025browsecompsimplechallengingbenchmark}. We propose that we can entirely replace SFT training of agentic capabilities by using a RL pipeline \textit{grounded} in the strengths of SFT \textit{without} the weaknesses of it. We first propose that any SFT dataset can be used for on-policy GRPO by using SFT prefixes as the input and the SFT completions as a verifier. We then propose a easy way to create such prefixes and verifiers by pointing out that agentic trajectories can be abstracted into an episodic MDP where each state is the result of tool-call.

Specifically, our contribution is as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We show that any SFT dataset can be formulated into a GRPO setting by showing that the SFT verifier can be used as an RLVR verifier, and propose the resulting PivotRL method for consuming SFT data for on-policy RL training.
    \item We demonstrate that any agentic SFT dataset can formatted into the PivotRL setting by establishing long-horizon agentic trajectories as eposodic MDPs with distinct states (called `pivots') separated by tool call actions.
    \item We examine different dataset filtering strategies to choose a strong RL dataset from the existing SFT data, and show that PivotRL can improve \texttt{Qwen3-30B-A3b-Thinking-2507} from $44.35$ accuracy in $\tau^2$-Bench to $63.81$ in the best setting, which is better than the $58.44$ accuracy that is achievable by SFT.
    \item We show that PivotRL can serve as a general training strategy for a variety of agentic capabilities, with meaningful results in $\tau^2$-Bench \citep{barres2025tau2bench}, SWE-Bench \cite{jimenez2024swebench}, Terminal Bench \citep{tbench_2025}, and BrowseComp \citep{wei2025browsecompsimplechallengingbenchmark}.
\end{itemize}
